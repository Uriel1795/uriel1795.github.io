Robots don't survive the season

Every spring the same thing happens. The robot comes apart, the parts go back in the bin, and next year's team builds something new out of them. Within a few weeks there's nothing left of a build that took eight months except a few blurry photos on somebody's phone.

That's what this is for. It's an archive of the robots built in this program — each one in 3D, so you can turn it around and look at how it was actually put together, alongside the engineering notebook and whatever the team wants to keep with it.

It exists because those robots deserve better than a bin. A team spends a season solving a genuinely hard problem, and the solution is thrown away as soon as it stops being useful. Meanwhile the next team starts from scratch on a brand new problem.

An eighth grader should be able to look at what a team built in 2021 and understand how they solved it.

What goes in it

Robots, season by season, team by team. A 3D model you can orbit, taken from CAD where the team modelled it and from photogrammetry where they didn't. The judges notebook if there is one. And mementos — the first drivetrain that got scrapped in October, the pit table at states, the bracket someone bent by hand on competition morning. Those last things are often what people actually remember.

Nothing appears until after Worlds, or until the team has shown the robot publicly themselves. Nobody should have to choose between contributing to this and protecting a competitive advantage.

How it's built

  • No student names or faces The archive is about the robots. Publishing photos of minors on a public website creates problems that aren't mine to create, so it doesn't. If you want to know who built something, ask them.
  • Nothing is collected No accounts, no sign-ups, no tracking, no email addresses stored. Submitting doesn't require telling me who you are.
  • Anything comes down on request No reason needed. See contact.
  • Built to outlast me Plain files, open formats, no proprietary viewer. If this project stops being maintained, the models are still .glb files anybody can open.

Who's behind it

I'm Cristian U. Polanco. I was born in the Dominican Republic and came here at fifteen without speaking English, so I learned it after I arrived. I studied at DeVry University and graduated with a bachelor's in Computer Information Systems, concentrating in information security.

Since then I've been at Robot Revolution, where I'm one of the lead programmers and help run the IT side. I also teach — VEX from third grade up, VEX IQ to elementary and middle schoolers, and C++ to seventh graders and older so they can move into our VEX V5 program.

Coaching is the part I'd keep if I could only keep one. Getting to teach something I'm this passionate about, to kids who are figuring out they're good at it, is a genuinely good way to spend your time. This archive exists because I got tired of watching that work disappear every spring.

Built and maintained by one person, in evenings and weekends. It is a personal project, it is not an official Robot Revolution website, it is not an official VEX or REC Foundation project, and it isn't run by any school or district. If something here should be different, the fastest way to change it is to say so.
